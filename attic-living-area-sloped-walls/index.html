Attic living area from the scans

An attic room counts less living area where its ceiling slopes down. LumoCad now works that out on its own from the laser scans. Where the scans are not enough, the architect draws the slope with two clicks in the 360° photo. Nobody types a height or an angle.

The problem in one minute

German living area (WoFlV, the Wohnflächenverordnung) counts a room's floor by how much headroom it has, measured straight up from the floor to the ceiling:

2 m or morecounts in full
1 m to under 2 mcounts half
under 1 mdoes not count

So the only two things that matter in a sloped room are where the ceiling is exactly 1 m high and where it is exactly 2 m high. Everything in front of the 1 m line is lost, between the lines half counts, behind the 2 m line all of it.

LumoCad used to read those heights from a modelled 3D roof. Since the switch to a 2D-only workflow (4 Nov 2026) roofs are not modelled any more, yet clients still pay for the living-area figure. Until now the architects produced it by hand in Revit: cut a section through the room, find the 1 m and 2 m lines, project them onto the plan, hatch each zone. The work described here does that cut for them.

What has been done, in simple terms

Three pieces, built on top of each other. The first is the shared base; the other two are the two ways of filling it in.

The base: a room remembers which of its walls the ceiling slopes down to

Every room can now carry a short list of sloped walls. Each entry names one wall of the room and gives two numbers: the knee height (how high the ceiling still is where it meets that wall; the low wall under a roof is the "Drempel") and the pitch (how steep the roof is, in degrees). From those two numbers LumoCad draws the ceiling, finds the 1 m and 2 m lines, colours the plan and prints the WoFlV figure. The list is saved with the room, loads with the building and comes back with Ctrl+Z like any other edit. In the right panel it appears as the list “Sloped walls”, one row per wall, next to the figure “WoFlV from sloped walls”.

Two shortcuts make real roofs fit this simple model. First, knee 0 is the drawing rule: the room books put the inner roof surface on the floor at the inner face of the façade, so that is where our slope starts too. Second, a roof with two pitches (a mansard, as in Nürnberg) is replaced by one equivalent plane that passes through the same 1 m and 2 m points. WoFlV cannot tell the difference, so neither do we.

Approach A automatic

From the scans alone. Nothing is typed. The architect only looks at the result and presses apply.

Two kinds of scans exist on every floor. A level scan is the lidar turned flat at the scanner's height: it shows where the beam hit walls, and where it hit the sloping ceiling instead. A slice is the lidar turned vertical: it shows floor, wall and the roof profile like a cross-section drawing.

  1. Lay the scans over the drawing. Scans were sorted in another coordinate system than the drawn rooms. The fit uses the scanner positions against the room centres, then nudges until scan points sit on the room walls.
  2. Read the roof from the slices. Floor level, the roof pitch (the most common slope line, not the steepest, which is usually a stair), and the distance from the wall at which the roof passes 1 m and 2 m.
  3. Tell a wall from a slope. A wall is a sharp line in the same place in every scan. A slope hit is smeared and moves with the scanner height. A wardrobe front is sharp too, but the slice shows the roof going on behind it.
  4. Find the façade and extend rooms drawn too short. Flat rooms (bath, kitchen) touch the outer wall and tell where it is. In Frankfurt the sloped rooms had been traced where the beam met the slope, 35–55 % too small; they are extended out to the façade.
  5. Set the slopes. One slope per façade wall, knee 0, the equivalent pitch through the 1 m and 2 m points. Rooms the roof does not cut get nothing.

The result is a proposal: per room, sloped yes/no, a confidence (measured from its own slice, or estimated from neighbours on the same side), the new outline, the slopes, the living area and a sentence saying why. One real example of that sentence:

“slope-like line (±48 mm in a scan, 95 mm between scans), roof 1404 mm there, slices see the slope down to 1504 mm → extend to the façade”

In the app it is a small panel at the top of the right sidebar while the boundary tool is active: “Sloped walls from scans” with Propose sloped walls (reads the active floor, lists every room with its reading, changes nothing), Apply to N rooms (writes it through the editor's own edit events, saved, as one undo step) and Clear slopes (empties the sloped walls of the whole floor, also one undo step, so a test can start from scratch; extended outlines stay). The same functions run as Calvin tools (Calvin is the command-line helper that talks to the LumoCad tab): room-sloped-walls-propose, room-sloped-walls-propose-apply and room-sloped-walls-reset. A tool for Marvin, the chat assistant, is not built yet.

Approach B draw it

The fallback: draw the slope in the 360° view, the same way boundaries are drawn today. For rooms the scans cannot read (no slice, furniture in the way, an odd roof), or to correct the automatic guess.

  1. Press “Draw” next to a wall in the “Sloped walls” list. In the 360° photo of the room that wall lights up pink.
  2. Click on the wall where the slope starts. The click is read against the wall plane, so it gives the knee height. A pink line previews it while the pointer moves, and the panel shows the live height.
  3. Click on the ceiling where the slope ends. That point lies on the flat ceiling, whose height is known. Knee height, ceiling height and the distance between the two clicks give the pitch. The panel shows the live angle.

The slope is saved like a typed one: the wall's switch goes on, Knee height and Pitch fill in, the WoFlV figure and the coloured plan zones update. Esc or Stop cancels. Dragging the camera still works, because only a click without movement counts.

If the automatic proposal already set a slope on that wall, the preview starts from it. Correcting a wrong guess is then one or two clicks, not a drawing from scratch.

Where it helps

  • Dormer rooms like the Frankfurt 88 living room: the automatic read says flat, the architect marks the two short sloped pieces beside the dormer.
  • Rooms without a usable slice, or with a wardrobe or boxes against the low wall.
  • Odd roofs: a steeper second wing, a loggia, a party wall the scans cannot explain.
  • Checking: two clicks on a room the automatic run already did show at once whether its guess sits where the real slope is.

What it does not do: it still describes one wall with one knee height and one pitch. A ceiling that bends twice is entered as the equivalent single plane, just as the automatic run does.

How the two fit together. The automatic proposal fills in every room it can read. The architect looks at the coloured zones on the plan and, where something looks wrong, draws over it with two clicks. Anything a person set wins: a drawn or typed slope replaces the automatic one, a hand-entered WoFlV figure beats both, and a room's own slopes beat a modelled roof above it.

Where it is still off. Nürnberg's two big rooms miss by about 3,8 m² each in opposite directions. That roof has two wings and knee walls, and one wing may be steeper than the pitch the slices agree on; the flat total happens to come out right, the rooms do not. Frankfurt 88's living room reads as flat while the book takes 6 % off: the room sits under a wide dormer, and dormers inside a sloped room are outside what this version detects. Frankfurt 91's Zimmer 1 is 1,7 m² short because its floor area is already 1,65 m² short, which points at the façade line, not at the slope.

What else was checked. Eight worked examples with hand-calculable numbers (floor fit, roof pitch, wall or slope, slice to plane, a room traced short, a wardrobe front, flat rooms with a balcony, a two-pitch roof). Seven rules on 30 random synthetic floors with a known roof: the same input always gives the same answer, a room is never shrunk, nothing is invented on a flat floor, an extension never crosses the façade or enters a neighbour, every slope is valid, uncut rooms keep their area, and the living area stays within 5 % of the true roof's. One random floor is 0,04 m² over that last tolerance and is accepted as is.

How to test

Three ways, from the most automatic to the most manual. The in-app steps use the dev copy of Frankfurt (Oder), Fürstenwalder Poststraße 88 (dev building id starting 9b4bceac, same id as in prod). It is on the Calvin allow-list, so tools may write to it.

A. The automatic proposal, from the sidebar panel

Open the building in LumoCad with Floor 4 active and the boundary tool selected (the mode in which you move room outline nodes). The right sidebar then starts with the panel “Sloped walls from scans”.

  1. Press Clear slopes first if you want to start from scratch: every room on the floor loses its sloped walls, the WoFlV figures go back to the plain floor areas. One undo step.
  2. Press Propose sloped walls. Nothing is written. Expect a summary like “4 of 8 room(s) sloped (4 measured, 0 estimated), …” and one row per room with its reading (flat, measured or estimated), floor area before → after when it would be extended, living area, and the first why-sentence (the full reasoning is in the row's tooltip). Zimmer 1, Zimmer 2, Schlafzimmer and the small WC should be sloped; Flur, Küche, Bad and Wohnzimmer flat.
  3. Press Apply to N rooms. Expect the line “Saved: N room(s): … · Ctrl+Z takes it back.” and the list to refresh with nothing left to apply.
  4. Select DG04 M Zimmer1. In the room section of the sidebar, “WoFlV from sloped walls” should read about 13,5 m² (room book 13,59). In the floor plan, switch the WoFlV area zones on: red, yellow and green strips along the façade.
  5. Press Ctrl+Z once: outlines and slopes go back together. Apply again and reload the page: the saved result is still there.

The same three steps from a terminal, through Calvin, with the dev server running and the tab open on the building:

pnpm calvin tool room-sloped-walls-reset            # start from scratch (slopes only)
pnpm calvin tool room-sloped-walls-propose          # look, change nothing
pnpm calvin tool room-sloped-walls-propose-apply    # apply, one undo step
One caveat for this building. Floor 4 of the dev copy was already corrected by hand on 17 Nov (outlines extended to the façade, slopes set). There the proposal mostly confirms what is saved. To watch it extend short outlines, copy Nr. 91 (prod id starting 4e6b5984) to dev and add it to the allow-list, or undo the 17 Nov edits first.

B. Drawing the slope in the 360° view

Works on any building that has 360° photos. A good first room: the same dev copy, Floor 4, DG04 M Zimmer1, its façade wall. Expect a knee height near 0 to 0,2 m and a pitch near 37°.

  1. Open the 360° view of a scan standing in the room and select the room, so its outline shows in the photo.
  2. In the right panel, find the list “Sloped walls” and press Draw on the façade wall. The wall lights up pink in the photo. The hint under the row reads “In the 360° view of this room: click on wall N where the slope starts” and shows the height under the pointer.
  3. Click on the wall where the slope begins, at the top of the low wall. The hint changes to “Now click on the ceiling where the slope ends” and shows the angle under the pointer.
  4. Click on the ceiling where the slope meets the flat ceiling. The wall's switch goes on, Knee height and Pitch fill in, “WoFlV from sloped walls” and the plan zones update.
  5. Try the escape routes: Esc or Stop cancels a drawing; dragging the camera is not a click; Ctrl+Z takes a saved slope back.
